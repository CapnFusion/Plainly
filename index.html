    How Plainly works, and where it can be wrong

    The score

    Plainly uses the Flesch Reading Ease formula, a standard readability measure since 1948: 206.835 − 1.015 × (words ÷ sentences) − 84.6 × (syllables ÷ words). Shorter sentences and shorter words give a higher score. A score of 60 to 70 is plain English, and below 30 is graduate-level writing.

    The five hardest sentences

    Each sentence gets a difficulty rating out of 100 from four signals: how long it is, how many long words (three or more syllables) it uses, how many syllables its words average, and how many commas, brackets and dashes break it up. Very short fragments are scaled down. The five highest ratings are marked in your text, and each one lists why it was flagged.

    Limits

    • The score estimates how hard text is to read. It does not measure whether readers will understand your ideas, or whether the writing is good.
    • Syllables are counted with rules of thumb, so a few words will be off by one.
    • Sentences are split at full stops, question marks and exclamation marks. Headings without punctuation, bullet lists and unusual abbreviations can confuse the splitter.
    • The rules are built for English. Other languages will give unreliable results.
    • Technical writing for experts can score as difficult and still be exactly right for its readers.
